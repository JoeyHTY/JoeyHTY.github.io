Research Interests

I study magnetized plasma around compact objects from two complementary directions: polarimetry and rotation-measure analysis of fast radio bursts (FRBs), which probe their magneto-ionic environments, and magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) simulations of jets, winds and shocks, which I am developing through my MSc thesis. My long-term goal is to connect polarized radio observations with a physical picture of the magnetic fields and plasma that produce them.

Current Projects

1. Relativistic simulations of standing shocks in the SS 433 jets (MSc thesis, Sept. 2026 – expected July 2027)
Developing relativistic BHAC simulations to understand why standing shocks form near the observed re-brightening regions of the SS 433 jets, testing the roles of disk-wind termination shocks, the W50 ambient medium and magnetic fields. Supervisor: Prof. Oliver Porth; day-to-day advisers: Laura Olivera-Nieto and Joana Kramer.

2. Polarimetry and burst energetics of FRB 20240114A (June 2026 – present)
Testing whether linear-polarization fraction, polarization-angle behaviour and rotation measure differ between lower- and higher-energy bursts, while controlling for calibration and selection effects. Manuscript in preparation. Supervisor: Prof. Jason Hessels.

Past Projects

3. Polarization-calibration diagnostics for FRB 20240114A (MSc Astrovaria project, 2025–2026)
Investigated whether apparent rotation-measure evolution is astrophysical or instrumental, using QU fitting and RM synthesis. Showed that instrumental differential delay can mimic rotation-measure shifts.

4. Magnetically driven winds from thin discs (BSc thesis, Chongqing University, 2025)
Built and compared two-dimensional axisymmetric MHD disk-wind models with PLUTO to quantify how initial magnetic-field strength affects mass loading and energy and momentum transport.

5. Detector physics for rare-event searches (2024–2025)
Analysed light-sensor data for the XeBrA xenon breakdown apparatus at UC Berkeley, and studied photoionization corrections for the XENONnT dark-matter experiment at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen.
